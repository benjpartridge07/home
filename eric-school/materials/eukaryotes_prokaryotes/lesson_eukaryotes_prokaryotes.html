Eukaryotes, prokaryotes & the scale of cells

Biology Maths 55 minutes
01

Eukaryotic cells

8 min

Animal and plant cells are called eukaryotic cells. Every eukaryotic cell has a cell membrane, cytoplasm, and genetic material enclosed in a nucleus. You already know the individual parts from the Animal & Plant Cells lesson — the key new word here is just the category name: eukaryotic means "has a nucleus."

02

Prokaryotic cells (bacteria)

10 min

Bacterial cells are called prokaryotic cells. They are much smaller than animal or plant cells, and simpler:

  • They have cytoplasm and a cell membrane, surrounded by a cell wall.
  • Their genetic material is not enclosed in a nucleus — it's a single loop of DNA floating free in the cytoplasm.
  • They may also have one or more small rings of DNA called plasmids.

Where the names come from: eukaryotic means "true nucleus" and prokaryotic means "before nucleus." That's the whole distinction in a nutshell — animal and plant cells keep their DNA sealed inside a nuclear membrane, while a bacterial cell's DNA is just one circular strand sitting loose in the cytoplasm, with no membrane around it at all.

Plasmids are small circular pieces of DNA, separate from the main DNA loop:

  • They're much smaller than the main loop and usually carry only a few genes, not the bacterium's full genetic blueprint.
  • They often carry genes that give the bacterium an advantage in certain conditions — the classic example is antibiotic resistance.
  • Plasmids can be copied and passed between bacteria independently of the main DNA loop, which is part of why antibiotic resistance can spread between bacteria.
  • This is also why bacteria are so useful in genetic engineering: a plasmid can be removed, cut open with enzymes, have a foreign gene inserted, and put back into a bacterium so it produces something useful — e.g. human insulin.

03

The scale of cells

15 min

Cells are measured in units far smaller than a metre. Each step down this ladder is ×1000 smaller (and each step up is ÷1000):

This is the zone that matters for cells: bacteria are about 1–5 µm across; animal and plant cells are about 10–100 µm across. That's why bacteria count as "much smaller" — they're roughly 10× smaller than a typical body cell.

Worked conversion: a cell is 0.02 mm wide. To convert mm → µm, multiply by 1000: 0.02 × 1000 = 20 µm. Going back the other way, µm → mm, divide by 1000.

You'll also sometimes see centimetres (cm) — 1 cm = 10 mm. Cells are never measured in cm; it's only mentioned here because the exam skill list includes it.

04

Standard form for tiny numbers

10 min

Cell sizes in metres are awkward decimals, so exam questions often ask for them in standard form: A × 10n, where A is between 1 and 10.

Worked example: a bacterium is 2 µm across. In metres, 2 µm = 0.000002 m. Counting the places, that's 2 × 10−6 m.

Worked example: an animal cell is 25 µm across. 25 µm = 0.000025 m = 2.5 × 10−5 m.

Shortcut: 1 µm = 10−6 m, so to convert a µm value straight to standard form metres, just attach ×10−6 and adjust so the first digit is between 1 and 10.

05

Order of magnitude calculations

10 min

An "order of magnitude" just means a factor of 10. If one thing is roughly 10 times bigger than another, they differ by one order of magnitude.

Worked example: a bacterium is about 2 µm across. An animal cell is about 20 µm across.

20 µm ÷ 2 µm = 10 — the animal cell is 10 times bigger, i.e. one order of magnitude bigger than the bacterium.

06

GCSE-style review questions

15 min

1. State two ways a prokaryotic (bacterial) cell is different from a eukaryotic (animal) cell.

[2 marks]
Any two of: bacterial cells are much smaller (1); genetic material is a single DNA loop, not enclosed in a nucleus (1); bacterial cells may have plasmids (1).

2. A plant cell is 0.03 mm wide. Convert this to micrometres.

[2 marks]
0.03 × 1000 (1)   = 30 µm (1)

3. A bacterium has a diameter of 5 µm. Write this diameter in metres, using standard form.

[2 marks]
5 µm = 0.000005 m (1)   = 5 × 10−6 m (1)

4. A red blood cell is about 7 µm across and a certain virus is about 0.07 µm across. How many times bigger is the red blood cell, and what is this as an order of magnitude?

[2 marks]
7 ÷ 0.07 = 100 times bigger (1)   two orders of magnitude (1)

5. Explain why a bacterial cell is described as prokaryotic rather than eukaryotic.

[1 mark]
Its genetic material is not enclosed in a nucleus (it's a single DNA loop) (1).

Tip: whenever you see µm in a question, the safe first move is to convert it to metres in standard form (×10−6) before doing anything else — most marks are lost from mixing up units, not from the maths itself.
