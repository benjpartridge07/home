Atomic structure

Physics Maths 60 minutes
01

What's inside an atom

10 min

Every atom is built from three sub-atomic particles: protons and neutrons packed into a tiny central nucleus, with electrons occupying the mostly-empty space around it in shells.

The nucleus is incredibly small compared to the whole atom — if the atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea, but it holds almost all of the atom's mass.

An atom on its own has no overall charge — the number of electrons always equals the number of protons, so the positive and negative charges cancel out.

02

Atomic number, mass number, and isotopes

15 min

Two numbers tell you everything about an atom's make-up:

  • Atomic number — the number of protons. This is what makes an element that element; every atom of a given element has the same atomic number.
  • Mass number — the total number of protons and neutrons added together.

From those two numbers you can work out all three particle counts:

protons = atomic number

electrons = atomic number (in a neutral atom)

neutrons = mass number − atomic number

Worked example: chlorine-35 has atomic number 17 and mass number 35. So it has 17 protons, 17 electrons, and 35 − 17 = 18 neutrons.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same atomic number, same number of protons) that have different numbers of neutrons — and therefore different mass numbers. Because they have the same electron arrangement, isotopes of an element react chemically in the same way, but they can have different physical properties (like mass).

03

Electron shells

15 min

Electrons don't sit anywhere they like — they occupy fixed energy levels (shells) around the nucleus, filling the lowest available shell first before starting the next one out.

For the elements you'll meet at GCSE (atomic number 1–20), the shells fill up in the pattern 2, 8, 8: 2 electrons in the first shell, up to 8 in the second, up to 8 in the third.

Worked example: sodium has atomic number 11, so it has 11 electrons. Fill the shells from the inside out: 2, then 8, then whatever's left → 2, 8, 1.

04

How the model of the atom changed over time

10 min

The atom hasn't always been pictured this way — the model changed as new evidence came in. You don't need to remember dates or names, but you should be able to describe the sequence and explain why each change happened:

  • Solid sphere — atoms thought to be tiny solid balls that couldn't be split up.
  • Plum pudding model — once electrons were discovered, the atom was pictured as a ball of positive charge with electrons embedded in it, like fruit in a pudding.
  • Nuclear model — an experiment fired particles at thin gold foil. Most passed straight through, but a few bounced almost straight back — which only made sense if the positive charge (and most of the mass) was concentrated in a tiny central nucleus, with mostly empty space around it.
  • Neutrons added — later experiments showed atomic masses were too big to be explained by protons alone, leading to the discovery of the neutron in the nucleus.

The key skill for exam questions here is explaining how new evidence changed the model — not just reciting the timeline.

05

GCSE-style review questions

15 min

1. State the relative charge of a proton, a neutron, and an electron.

[3 marks]
Proton +1 (1)   Neutron 0 (1)   Electron −1 (1)

2. An atom of potassium has atomic number 19 and mass number 39. State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons it has.

[3 marks]
Protons = 19 (1)   Electrons = 19 (1)   Neutrons = 39 − 19 = 20 (1)

3. Define the term "isotope".

[2 marks]
Atoms of the same element (same number of protons) (1) with a different number of neutrons (different mass number) (1).

4. Give the electronic structure of an atom with atomic number 13.

[2 marks]
13 electrons, filling 2 then 8 then the remainder (1)   2,8,3 (1)

5. Describe how the alpha particle scattering experiment changed the plum pudding model of the atom.

[3 marks]
Most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil, showing atoms are mostly empty space (1). A small number bounced back at large angles, showing that a small, dense, positively charged region exists (1). This led to the nuclear model, with a tiny central nucleus containing (almost) all the mass and positive charge (1).

Tip: for "describe/explain" questions on the atomic model, always link the observation (what the experiment showed) to the conclusion (what it proved about atomic structure) — marks are usually awarded for that link, not just for naming the model.
