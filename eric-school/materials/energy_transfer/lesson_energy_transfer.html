Energy Transfer

Physics Maths (formula substitution & rearranging) 90 minutes
01

Energy stores and systems

8 min

A system is just an object, or a group of objects, that you're looking at. Energy is always stored in one or more of eight ways — when something changes (a ball falls, a kettle heats up, a spring stretches), energy moves between these stores, but the total amount never changes.

Kineticanything moving
Gravitational potentialanything raised up, in a gravitational field
Elastic potentialanything stretched or squashed (springs)
Thermalanything with a temperature above absolute zero
Chemicalfuels, food, batteries
Nuclearthe nucleus of atoms
Electrostaticcharges that attract/repel at a distance
Magneticmagnets that attract/repel at a distance

Example: a ball held above the ground stores energy gravitationally. Once dropped, that store empties as its kinetic store fills up — the energy hasn't vanished, it's just moved between stores.

02

Transfers, conservation & dissipation

10 min

Energy moves between stores along four possible pathways:

  • Mechanically — a force acting through a distance (pushing, pulling, stretching)
  • Electrically — charge moving through a circuit
  • By heating — energy transferred because of a temperature difference
  • By radiation — light or sound waves spreading out from a source

Conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed — it can only be transferred, stored, or dissipated. The total energy of a closed system is always the same before and after any change.

Dissipation is what happens to "wasted" energy — it spreads out into the surroundings, usually heating them up, and becomes increasingly difficult to use for anything useful. It isn't gone, it's just spread too thinly to be useful — that's why we call it "wasted" rather than "destroyed".

03

Kinetic & gravitational potential energy

12 min

kinetic energy = ½ × mass × speed²   or   Ek = ½mv²

Ek — kinetic energy, in joules, J  ·  m — mass, in kg  ·  v — speed, in m/s. Notice the speed is squared — doubling an object's speed doesn't double its kinetic energy, it quadruples it.

Worked example: a 2 kg ball moving at 3 m/s.   Ek = ½ × 2 × 3² = ½ × 2 × 9 = 9 J

gravitational potential energy = mass × gravitational field strength × height   or   Ep = mgh

Ep — GPE, in J  ·  m — mass, in kg  ·  g — gravitational field strength, 9.8 N/kg near Earth's surface  ·  h — height, in m

Worked example: a 5 kg mass raised 2 m.   Ep = 5 × 9.8 × 2 = 98 J

04

Elastic potential energy & specific heat capacity

12 min

elastic potential energy = ½ × spring constant × extension²   or   Ee = ½ke²   (assuming the spring hasn't gone past its limit of proportionality)

k — spring constant, in N/m  ·  e — extension, in m. Worked example: k = 40 N/m, e = 0.2 m.   Ee = ½ × 40 × 0.2² = ½ × 40 × 0.04 = 0.8 J

change in thermal energy = mass × specific heat capacity × change in temperature   or   ΔE = mcΔθ

ΔE — energy transferred, in J  ·  m — mass, in kg  ·  c — specific heat capacity, in J/kg°C (a property of the material — water is 4200)  ·  Δθ — temperature change, in °C

Worked example: 2 kg of water, c = 4200 J/kg°C, heated through 10°C.   ΔE = 2 × 4200 × 10 = 84,000 J = 8.4 × 10⁴ J — a good chance to reuse standard form from Maths.

05

Power and efficiency

12 min

power = energy transferred ÷ time   or   P = E / t   — power is measured in watts, W (1 W = 1 J of energy transferred per second).

Worked example: a motor transfers 600 J in 5 s.   P = 600 / 5 = 120 W

efficiency = useful output energy transfer ÷ total input energy transfer   (×100 to give a percentage)

Because some energy is always dissipated (usually as heat, through friction or electrical resistance), efficiency is always less than 1 (or less than 100%) for a real machine — it's impossible to build a machine that wastes nothing.

Worked example: a lamp is supplied with 500 J, and usefully transfers 350 J as light.   efficiency = 350 / 500 = 0.7 = 70%

Common ways to reduce unwanted energy transfers: lubrication (oil/grease reduces friction between moving parts) and insulation (reduces unwanted heating transfers, e.g. loft insulation in a house).

06

GCSE-style review questions

15 min

1. State the principle of conservation of energy.

[1 mark]
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred, stored or dissipated (1).

2. Name the four ways that energy can be transferred.

[4 marks]
Mechanically (1), electrically (1), by heating (1), by radiation (1).

3. Calculate the kinetic energy of a 1200 kg car travelling at 15 m/s.

[2 marks]
Ek = ½mv² = ½ × 1200 × 15² (1)   Ek = 135,000 J (1.35 × 10⁵ J) (1)

4. A spring with a spring constant of 25 N/m is stretched by 0.08 m. Calculate its elastic potential energy. Show your working.

[3 marks]
Ee = ½ke² (1)   Ee = ½ × 25 × 0.08² (1)   Ee = 0.08 J (1)

5. 2 kg of water gains 42,000 J of energy when heated. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J/kg°C. Calculate the temperature rise. Show how you rearranged the equation.

[3 marks]
Δθ = ΔE / (mc) (1)   Δθ = 42000 / (2 × 4200) (1)   Δθ = 5°C (1)

6. A kettle has a power rating of 2000 W. Calculate the energy it transfers when used for 3 minutes.

[3 marks]
3 minutes = 180 s (1)   E = Pt = 2000 × 180 (1)   E = 360,000 J (3.6 × 10⁵ J) (1)

7. A lamp has a total energy input of 60 J, and produces 12 J of useful light energy (the rest is wasted as heat). Calculate its efficiency as a percentage.

[2 marks]
efficiency = 12 / 60 (1)   efficiency = 0.2 = 20% (1)

8. Explain, in terms of energy, why no real machine can ever be 100% efficient, and give one way of reducing unwanted energy transfers caused by friction.

[3 marks]
Some of the input energy is always dissipated to the surroundings, usually as heat, due to friction/resistance (1). This wasted energy can't be usefully recovered, so useful output energy transfer is always less than total input energy transfer (1). Lubrication (oiling/greasing moving parts) reduces friction and unwanted heat transfer (1) — insulation is also accepted for a heat-transfer context.

Tip: on calculation questions, write the equation first, then the substituted numbers, then the final answer with units — that's usually 3 separate marks even if the arithmetic slips at the end.
