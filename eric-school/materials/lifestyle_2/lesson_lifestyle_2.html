Hormones, blood glucose & diabetes

Biology 50 minutes
01

The endocrine system

8 min

The endocrine system is made up of glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream.

A hormone is a chemical messenger. It travels in the blood until it reaches its target organ, where it produces an effect.

  • Pituitary gland (in the brain) — often called the "master gland" because it releases hormones that control several other glands
  • Pancreas — releases insulin and glucagon, which control blood glucose concentration (the focus of this lesson)
  • Adrenal glands — release adrenaline, preparing the body for "fight or flight"
  • Ovaries / testes — release reproductive hormones
02

Hormones vs nerves

6 min

The body has two coordination systems that respond to change: the nervous system (electrical impulses along neurons) and the hormonal (endocrine) system (chemical messengers in the blood). Both are used to keep conditions in the body constant — part of homeostasis.

FeatureNervous systemHormonal system
SignalElectrical impulses along neuronsChemicals (hormones) carried in the blood
SpeedVery fastSlower
EffectShort-lived, acts on a precise areaLonger-lasting, can act on a wide area
ExamplePulling your hand off something hotInsulin controlling blood glucose
03

Controlling blood glucose concentration

12 min

Blood glucose concentration is monitored and controlled by the pancreas, as part of homeostasis (recap: Part 1).

After eating carbohydrate: blood glucose concentration rises above normal → the pancreas releases insulin into the blood → insulin causes cells (especially liver and muscle cells) to take up glucose from the blood, using some for respiration and converting the excess into glycogen for storage → blood glucose concentration falls back to normal.

Beyond Foundation — the other direction

When blood glucose is too low (e.g. after exercise or between meals), the pancreas releases glucagon, which causes glycogen stores in the liver to be converted back into glucose and released into the blood. Foundation tier only requires you to know about insulin, but glucagon completes the picture: together they keep blood glucose steady by negative feedback — whichever way the level moves, a hormone response pushes it back to normal.

04

Type 1 & Type 2 diabetes

14 min

Diabetes is a condition where the body cannot properly control blood glucose concentration. There are two types.

Type 1 diabetesType 2 diabetes
CauseThe pancreas produces little or no insulinBody cells stop responding properly to insulin (or the pancreas doesn't produce enough)
Usual onsetOften develops in childhoodUsually develops later in life, though increasingly seen in younger people too
Key risk factorsNot strongly linked to lifestyleStrongly linked to lifestyle — obesity, poor diet, lack of exercise
TreatmentRegular insulin injections, matched to diet and activity; monitoring blood glucoseLifestyle changes (healthier diet, more exercise, weight loss); medication; insulin if needed

Exam tip — same problem, different cause

Both types share the same underlying issue: blood glucose cannot be controlled properly, so it can stay too high (hyperglycaemia). Left unmanaged, this can damage blood vessels and nerves and increase the risk of further disease, e.g. cardiovascular disease.

05

Hormones, lifestyle & health risk

8 min

This links back to Part 1: obesity is a major risk factor for Type 2 diabetes, because carrying excess body fat makes it harder for cells to respond properly to insulin. A diet high in fat/sugar and a lack of exercise both increase the risk of obesity and directly increase the risk of Type 2 diabetes — genetics/family history also plays a role.

  • Managing Type 2 diabetes risk uses the same lifestyle changes that reduce cardiovascular disease risk: better diet, more exercise, maintaining a healthy weight
  • Someone with poorly controlled diabetes is also at greater risk of cardiovascular disease — risk factors and diseases can interact, not act in isolation

06

GCSE-style review questions

8 min

1. What is meant by the term "hormone"?

[2 marks]
A chemical messenger (1); produced by a gland and carried in the blood to a target organ, where it produces an effect (1).

2. Describe how insulin controls blood glucose concentration after a meal.

[3 marks]
The pancreas detects blood glucose has risen and releases insulin (1); insulin causes cells (e.g. liver/muscle) to take up glucose from the blood (1); excess glucose is converted to glycogen for storage, so blood glucose concentration falls back to normal (1).

3. State one difference between how the nervous system and the hormonal system send signals around the body.

[2 marks]
Any one linked pair: the nervous system uses electrical impulses along neurons, the hormonal system uses chemicals carried in the blood (1); nervous system responses are fast, hormonal responses are slower (1); accept nervous effects are short-lived/precise vs hormonal effects longer-lasting/widespread.

4. What causes Type 1 diabetes?

[2 marks]
The pancreas produces little or no insulin (1); so blood glucose concentration cannot be properly controlled/stays too high (1).

5. Explain why obesity increases the risk of developing Type 2 diabetes.

[2 marks]
Carrying excess body fat makes body cells less responsive to insulin (insulin resistance) (1); so blood glucose concentration is not controlled properly, increasing the risk of developing Type 2 diabetes (1).

6. Compare how Type 1 diabetes and Type 2 diabetes are treated.

[4 marks]
Type 1: treated with regular insulin injections (1), matched to diet and activity, alongside monitoring blood glucose (1). Type 2: treated mainly with lifestyle changes — healthier diet, more exercise, weight loss (1); medication, and insulin if needed, may also be used (1).

Tip: if a question asks about diabetes "risk," lead with the lifestyle link for Type 2 (diet, exercise, obesity) — Type 1 is not caused by lifestyle, so don't mix the two up in an exam answer.
