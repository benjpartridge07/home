01

Aerobic & anaerobic respiration

6 min

Every cell needs energy, and it gets that energy from respiration — the breakdown of glucose. Most of the time this happens aerobically (using oxygen):

glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water  (+ energy released)

But if your muscles can't get oxygen fast enough — like during a sprint — they switch to anaerobic respiration instead:

glucose → lactic acid  (+ energy released, no oxygen needed)

Anaerobic respiration is faster to kick in but releases far less energy per glucose molecule, and the lactic acid that builds up causes muscle fatigue and cramp.

02

Exchange surfaces & the alveoli

9 min

To respire, cells need a steady supply of oxygen in and carbon dioxide out. Every efficient exchange surface in the body shares the same four features:

  • Large surface area — lets a lot of a substance cross at once
  • Thin walls — short diffusion distance
  • Good blood supply — keeps a steep concentration gradient
  • Moist lining — lets gases dissolve so they can diffuse

alveolus (green) · capillary (blue)

  • Millions of tiny alveoli in the lungs give the gas exchange surface a huge surface area
  • Alveoli walls are just one cell thick — a short diffusion path for oxygen and CO₂
  • Each alveolus is wrapped in capillaries, giving a good blood supply that keeps the gradient steep
  • The lining is moist, so gases can dissolve and diffuse across
03

The heart & double circulation

9 min
deoxygenated (right side) oxygenated (left side)

right atrium & ventricle (left of diagram) · left atrium & ventricle (right of diagram)

  • Blood passes through the heart twice per full circuit — this is double circulation
  • Pulmonary circuit: right ventricle → lungs (to pick up oxygen) → back to the left atrium
  • Systemic circuit: left ventricle → the rest of the body → back to the right atrium
  • The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right — it has to pump blood all the way around the body, not just to the nearby lungs
04

Blood vessels

7 min

Artery

  • Thick, muscular & elastic wall — withstands high pressure
  • Narrow lumen
  • Carries blood away from the heart

Vein

  • Thin wall, wide lumen
  • Valves stop blood flowing backwards
  • Carries blood at low pressure to the heart

Capillary

  • Wall is just one cell thick
  • Links arteries to veins
  • Substances diffuse quickly into/out of nearby cells

06

GCSE-style review questions

11 min

1. Complete the word equation for aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen → ______ + ______.

[2 marks]
Carbon dioxide (1) + water (1).

2. A sprinter's muscles can't get enough oxygen during a race. Name the type of respiration this triggers, and explain one problem it causes.

[3 marks]
Anaerobic respiration (1); it produces lactic acid (1), which builds up and causes muscle fatigue/cramp (1).

3. Explain how the structure of alveoli makes them efficient at gas exchange.

[4 marks]
There are millions of alveoli, giving a large surface area (1); the walls are one cell thick, giving a short diffusion distance (1); they're surrounded by capillaries, giving a good blood supply that maintains a steep concentration gradient (1); the lining is moist, letting gases dissolve (1).

4. Explain why the left ventricle has a thicker, more muscular wall than the right ventricle.

[2 marks]
The left ventricle pumps blood all the way around the body (systemic circuit) (1), while the right ventricle only pumps blood to the nearby lungs, so the left needs to generate much higher pressure (1).

5. State one structural difference between an artery and a vein, and explain why it's needed.

[2 marks]
Arteries have thicker, more muscular/elastic walls (1) because they carry blood at high pressure away from the heart (1). [or: veins have a wider lumen (1) to help blood flow at low pressure (1)]

6. Explain how red blood cells are adapted to carry oxygen efficiently.

[3 marks]
Biconcave disc shape gives a large surface area (1); no nucleus, giving more space to carry oxygen (1); contains haemoglobin, which binds to oxygen (1).

Tip: whenever a question mentions "double circulation", remember it's really two loops sharing one heart — right side to the lungs, left side to the body — not blood going round twice in one loop.
